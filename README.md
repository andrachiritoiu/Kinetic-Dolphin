# Kinetic Dolphin

## Description

A dolphin that swims with a turn of a crank. A mechanical cam-based mechanism drives the smooth, wave-like motion of a dolphin's body — no electronics, no motors, just pure mechanics.

Designed from scratch using CAD software, this project explores how rotational motion can be converted into realistic animal movement through a series of cams and linkages mounted on a central shaft. As the crank turns, each cam is offset at a different angle, creating a fluid, sequential motion across the dolphin's body segments that mimics natural swimming.

---

## Components & Parts

| Part | Role |
|------|------|
| Crank handle | Input — the part you turn to drive the mechanism |
| Central shaft | Transfers rotation from the crank to all cams |
| Cams (x4) | Each offset at a different angle to create wave-like sequential motion |
| Body segments (x4) | Ride on top of the cams, rising and falling to simulate swimming |
| Dolphin head | Fixed front piece, attached to the frame |
| Tail fin | Last segment — has the largest range of motion |
| Frame / base | Holds the shaft, cams and body segments in alignment |

---


## Implementation Details and Challenges

The core of the mechanism is a central shaft with four cams, each rotated at a different phase angle (approximately 90° apart). As the shaft turns, each cam pushes its corresponding body segment upward at a slightly different moment, creating the illusion of a wave passing through the dolphin's body from head to tail.

The biggest design challenge was finding the right cam profile. A circular eccentric cam produces a smooth sinusoidal lift, while a more aggressive profile produces sharper, more exaggerated movement. Several iterations were needed to find the shape that looked most like natural dolphin swimming.

Tolerances were also critical — too tight and the segments bind on the cams; too loose and the motion looks sloppy. The frame had to be designed carefully to keep the shaft aligned under the lateral forces generated by the cams pushing against the segments.

The tail fin required special attention as it has the largest range of motion and is the most visually prominent part of the animation. Its geometry was designed to flex slightly rather than move as a rigid body, giving the motion a more organic feel.
